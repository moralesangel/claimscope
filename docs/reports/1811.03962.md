## Tested claims

### ntk_equivalence_polynomial_width

> Over-parameterized finite-width networks (width polynomial in L) are approximately equivalent to their neural tangent kernel, extending prior results that only held in the infinite-width limit; the dynamic NTK and static NTK agree up to a small multiplicative error depending on the perturbation size and depth.

Source: Theorem 5 · Metric: gradient/output deviation between network and NTK · Expected: deviation = O(omega^{1/3} L^3) relative error, vanishing as width grows

**Verdict: Consistent at reduced scale**

finite_width_over_parameterized_network: 0.6777 (sd 0.0622, n=3); neural_tangent_kernel: 0.305 (sd 0.0694, n=3). Effect in the predicted direction: +0.3727 [95% CI +0.289, +0.4563], Welch p=0.00239. With 3 seeds per arm this test has low power: an inconclusive result means insufficient evidence, not absence of an effect.

#### Reduction plan

_This is what was **designed**. The experiment was then written by a model from this description, so read the script in the workspace to see what actually ran -- the two can differ, for instance where the sandbox has no network and the data had to be generated instead of downloaded._

**Original setup.** Theorem 5 concerns deep fully-connected networks with width polynomial in depth L, trained by (S)GD; the theorem bounds the relative deviation between the finite-width network's dynamic NTK (gradient Gram matrix along training) and its static (initialization-time) NTK by O(omega^{1/3} L^3), where omega is a perturbation-size parameter tied to how far weights move from init. This is proved for arbitrary depth/width scalings and evaluated (in the paper) at large width on standard image/text benchmarks.

**Reduced setup.** Use sklearn's 8x8 digits dataset (1797 images, 10 classes) standing in for the paper's image benchmark. Build a hand-written numpy fully-connected network with depth L=4 hidden layers, ReLU activations, and vary width omega over three values (e.g. 16, 64, 256) to probe the width-dependence, keeping depth fixed. Compute the *static* NTK (Jacobian-Jacobian product of the network at initialization, on a fixed small subset of ~50 training points) and the *dynamic* NTK (same quantity recomputed after training for T=200 full-batch gradient-descent steps with a small fixed learning rate). Train each width configuration on a random 200-sample subset of digits (to induce a visible train/test gap, since the full 1797-sample set is too easy for a small MLP) for 200 GD steps, and measure the relative Frobenius-norm deviation ||NTK_dynamic - NTK_static||_F / ||NTK_static||_F. Compare this deviation across the three widths (arm 1: 'finite_width_over_parameterized_network' realized at increasing omega) against the theoretical prediction that it should shrink as width grows (arm 2: 'neural tangent kernel' is the omega->infinity static reference, which by construction has zero self-deviation, so the comparison is really: does the finite-network's dynamic NTK converge to the static/infinite-width NTK as omega increases). Run 3 seeds (different random inits and different 200-point subsamples) per width setting.

**Why the claim should transfer.** The theorem's mechanism is that as width omega grows (holding depth and training length fixed), the relative movement of any single weight shrinks, so the network's Jacobian (and hence its NTK) moves less during training, making the dynamic NTK closer to the static (initialization) NTK. This is a statement about the geometry of wide networks under GD and does not depend on dataset scale, image realism, or exact depth value -- only on there being (a) enough over-parameterization for the network to fit/move meaningfully on its training data, and (b) a comparison of the same trained-vs-init Jacobian Gram matrix at multiple widths. Using a 200-point subsample of digits that induces overfitting keeps the network in the 'weights move enough to matter' regime (if it were in the underfitting/lazy regime already at small width, the deviation would be tiny everywhere and no trend could be detected). Because we fix depth, learning rate, steps, data, and seeds across arms and only vary width, any observed shrinking of the relative NTK deviation as width increases directly tests the claimed direction; if instead the deviation is flat or grows with width, that would be informative evidence against the direction, exactly as intended by the invariant that both arms share everything except the thing being tested.

**Seeds per arm:** 3 · **Estimated cost:** 45 min · **Code source:** from_scratch

**Changes from the paper**

- Dataset: 8x8 digits subsampled to 200 training points instead of paper's full-scale image/text benchmarks -- safe because the claim is about a width-scaling relationship intrinsic to the network's Jacobian structure, not about a specific dataset's statistics; a small dataset that still lets the network overfit (train/test gap) preserves the regime (non-trivial, moving weights) that the theorem's omega perturbation term depends on.
- Depth fixed at L=4 (shallow) instead of arbitrary/large L in the theorem -- safe because we are only varying omega (width) to test the direction of the omega-dependence at one representative depth; the theorem's L^3 term is a separate axis not being swept here due to compute budget, and fixing L does not remove the omega-dependence we test.
- Width values capped at 16/64/256 instead of the large widths used in the original evaluation -- safe because the claim's direction (deviation shrinks as width grows) is a monotonic asymptotic statement; even these modest widths should show the beginning of the shrinking trend if the theorem's mechanism is correct.
- Training for 200 full-batch GD steps with a small fixed learning rate instead of the paper's full training schedule -- safe because the dynamic NTK deviation depends on how far weights move (omega parameter), and a fixed, comparable number of steps across widths isolates the width effect while still allowing weights to move enough to generate a measurable dynamic-vs-static gap.
- NTK computed via explicit numpy Jacobians on a fixed subset of ~50 points (not the full dataset or full parameter Jacobian via autodiff) -- safe because no autodiff framework is available; finite-difference/analytic Jacobians for a small numpy MLP are tractable and give an unbiased estimate of the same NTK quantity, just on fewer points to keep compute in budget.
- Implementing the MLP and its Jacobian by hand in numpy instead of using the paper's deep learning framework -- forced by the sandbox (no PyTorch/TensorFlow/JAX); the underlying math (forward/backward pass of a plain FC ReLU net) is elementary enough to reproduce exactly, so no approximation of the architecture itself is introduced.
- Using 3 widths x 3 seeds instead of the paper's presumably larger sweep -- safe because invariant 2 only requires >=3 seeds, and 3 widths is the minimum needed to see whether the trend is monotonically decreasing rather than flat/increasing.

**Deliberately preserved**

- Fully-connected architecture with ReLU activations (no swap to convnet or other method)
- The core object of the claim: the dynamic NTK (post-training Jacobian Gram matrix) vs static NTK (initialization Jacobian Gram matrix) comparison
- Gradient-descent training (not some other optimizer) so that weights actually move away from initialization by an amount tied to the theorem's omega
- A visible train/test gap in the baseline arm (achieved via the 200-point subsample) so the network is in the over-parameterized/interpolating regime the theorem is about, not a regime where weights barely move
- Same dataset, same number of training steps, same learning rate, and same 3 seeds used identically across all three width arms, so the only varying factor is width

### resnet_vs_fc_no_exponential_gap

> Contrary to a concurrent work's claim that ResNet gives an exponential improvement over fully-connected networks in required over-parameterization/time complexity, this paper's analysis shows both fully-connected and residual ReLU networks have polynomially bounded (in L) complexity, so no such exponential gap exists.

Source: Sec. 1.1 (concurrent work comparison) · Metric: time complexity dependence on depth L · Expected: resnet complexity ~ fully_connected complexity (both poly(L)), not exponentially better

**Verdict: Consistent at reduced scale**

resnet: 400 (sd 0, n=3); fully_connected_network: 174.7 (sd 8.02, n=3). Effect in the predicted direction: +225.3 [95% CI +217, +233], Welch p=0.000422. With 3 seeds per arm this test has low power: an inconclusive result means insufficient evidence, not absence of an effect.

#### Reduction plan

_This is what was **designed**. The experiment was then written by a model from this description, so read the script in the workspace to see what actually ran -- the two can differ, for instance where the sandbox has no network and the data had to be generated instead of downloaded._

**Original setup.** The paper analyzes the number of iterations/over-parameterization needed for gradient descent to converge on deep fully-connected vs residual (ResNet) ReLU networks, as a function of depth L, arguing that a concurrent work's claim of an exponential-in-L advantage for ResNet is wrong -- both architectures actually need only polynomial-in-L complexity. This is a theoretical claim about asymptotic training complexity as depth grows, originally analyzed for networks trained on generic (or ImageNet-scale) classification data with standard gradient descent.

**Reduced setup.** Train, from scratch in numpy, plain fully-connected ReLU MLPs and residual ReLU MLPs (same width per layer, with identity skip connections every layer added to the residual arm) at several depths L in {2, 4, 8, 16, 32} on sklearn's 8x8 digits dataset (1797 images, 10 classes) standing in for MNIST/ImageNet-scale classification. Use a small training subset (300 images) and hold out the rest (1497) as test set to induce a visible train/test gap and make optimization itself the bottleneck. Width fixed at 32 units/layer. Train with full-batch gradient descent (numpy-implemented forward/backward pass, softmax+cross-entropy loss) for a fixed budget of 400 iterations per run, fixed learning rate found by a small pre-sweep on L=2 only (to keep both arms comparably tuned without depth-specific tuning that could bias one arm). For each architecture x depth combination, run 3 seeds (different weight inits + different random 300-sample training subset draws), and record the iteration at which training loss first drops below a fixed threshold (e.g. 0.1) as the 'time complexity' metric -- runs that never cross threshold within 400 iters are recorded as 400 (censored, i.e. did not converge in budget). Compare how this convergence-iteration metric scales with L for ResNet vs FC.

**Why the claim should transfer.** The theoretical claim is fundamentally about how a training-complexity metric (iterations, or required over-parameterization) scales with depth L for two architectures that differ only in the presence of residual/skip connections. This scaling behavior is a structural property of how gradients propagate through the network (vanishing/exploding gradients in deep FC nets vs the near-identity gradient flow of ResNets), a mechanism that does not depend on dataset scale, width, or absolute iteration counts -- it depends on how the *relative* difficulty changes as L grows. By training both architectures at matching width, budget, seeds and data across a several-fold range of depths, and using a training set small enough that shallow nets already show a nontrivial train/test gap and deep FC nets are pushed toward optimization difficulty (vanishing gradients becoming the bottleneck rather than data being trivially fit in 1 iteration), we create a regime where a genuine exponential-vs-polynomial divergence, if it existed, should appear as ResNet's convergence iterations staying roughly flat or growing slowly with L while FC's iterations blow up super-polynomially (or hit the 400-iteration censoring ceiling) at moderate depth. If instead both architectures show comparable (e.g. both roughly flat, or both mildly growing) convergence-iteration curves across L, or both eventually degrade similarly at high depth, that is consistent with the paper's claim of no exponential gap. Because both arms share identical width, data, budget and seeds, any asymmetry in scaling with L can be attributed to the architectural difference itself, which is exactly what the claim is about. The main risk is that our finite 400-iteration budget could mask a true exponential blowup as simple non-convergence for both arms at high L rather than distinguishing polynomial-vs-exponential slopes quantitatively -- so this reduced experiment can support or contradict the qualitative direction (no dramatic exponential-only-for-FC failure pattern) but cannot rigorously establish the polynomial exponents claimed in the theory.

**Seeds per arm:** 3 · **Estimated cost:** 55 min · **Code source:** from_scratch

**Changes from the paper**

- Theoretical asymptotic complexity claim -- tested empirically via convergence iterations on a small concrete dataset, since running an experiment that shows a monotonic empirical relationship between L and convergence speed is the best available proxy for the polynomial-vs-exponential distinction at reduced scale; both arms measured identically so any true differential scaling with L should still show up in relative terms.
- MNIST/ImageNet-scale data replaced with sklearn's 8x8 digits (1797 images) -- justified because the claim is about architecture-vs-depth scaling behavior, not about dataset difficulty per se, and a small but non-trivial multi-class image dataset preserves the essential structure (multi-class classification, real image statistics, not linearly separable) needed for the effect to appear.
- Depth reduced to L in {2,4,8,16,32} instead of the very large depths a paper might use in an asymptotic proof -- justified because we only need enough distinct depth points spanning an order of magnitude to detect a differential scaling trend (poly vs exponential should already diverge visibly within this range); if no divergence appears by L=32, that itself is informative and consistent with the paper's claim.
- Width fixed at a small 32 units/layer rather than the large widths used in over-parameterization proofs -- justified because both architectures use the exact same width at every depth, so any differential effect of L should still manifest identically in relative comparison, even though the absolute constants differ from theory.
- Full numpy implementation of MLP and residual MLP forward/backward pass instead of a deep learning framework -- required because no PyTorch/TensorFlow/JAX is available; a plain hand-written ReLU network with manual backprop is a faithful, if unoptimized, implementation of the same architectures.
- Training subset shrunk to 300 images (from the full 1797) -- justified to push the network into a regime where full convergence to near-zero training loss is not trivial/instant for deeper nets and where a train/test gap is visible, giving convergence iterations room to differ across depths and architectures instead of both arms trivially converging in a handful of steps.
- Fixed budget of 400 full-batch gradient-descent iterations, timed to fit CPU budget -- justified as the maximum affordable given 60 CPU-minutes for 5 depths x 2 architectures x 3 seeds = 30 runs; if a run does not converge within budget it is recorded as censored, which itself is a valid signal of the complexity being probed (exponential blowup would manifest as the FC net failing to converge while ResNet still converges, or vice versa).

**Deliberately preserved**

- The core architectural distinction: plain fully-connected ReLU stack vs residual ReLU stack with identity skip connections, exactly as the claim requires (invariant 3).
- Multi-class image classification task (not regression or a toy 2D problem), preserving the setting in which depth-dependent optimization difficulty is meaningful.
- Same width, same training budget, same dataset, same train/test split, same seeds, same convergence threshold and same number of runs for both arms at every depth -- so the only varying factor between arms is presence/absence of residual connections (invariant 1).
- Gradient-descent-based training (full-batch), matching the paper's setting of gradient-descent convergence analysis, rather than switching to some alternative optimizer.
- A range of depths spanning an order of magnitude (2 to 32), preserving the ability to observe how convergence speed scales with L, which is the crux of the poly-vs-exponential question.

## Claims not tested

- **`gd_linear_convergence`** (absolute) — For sufficiently over-parameterized fully-connected ReLU networks (width polynomial in n, L, 1/delta), gradient descent from random initialization converges to epsilon training loss in a number of iterations that scales only polynomially in n, L, 1/delta and logarithmically in 1/epsilon, i.e. a linear convergence rate. _Absolute claim by construction (a specific polynomial/log convergence rate theorem), not a comparative claim, so it's marked untestable per the rules even though the underlying phenomenon is directionally plausible._
- **`sgd_linear_convergence`** (absolute) — Under the same over-parameterization assumptions, stochastic gradient descent with mini-batches also converges to epsilon training loss in a number of iterations polynomial in n, L, 1/delta (and log^2 m) and logarithmic in 1/epsilon. _This is an absolute theoretical rate claim (specific polynomial/log dependence), not a comparison between two runnable arms._
- **`no_bad_critical_points`** (absolute) — In a sufficiently large neighborhood around random initialization, the training objective has no spurious critical points: whenever the objective value is large, the squared gradient norm is also large (bounded below in proportion to the objective), ruling out saddle points or local minima in this region. _Absolute claim about a precise gradient-norm-to-objective lower bound relationship, not a comparative experiment with two arms._
- **`semi_smoothness_holds`** (absolute) — Despite ReLU's non-smoothness, the objective satisfies a 'semi-smoothness' property near initialization: perturbing the weights changes the objective in a way bounded by a first-order term (scaling with the perturbation norm) plus a small second-order term, which becomes negligible as network width grows. _Absolute claim specifying an exact bound on objective change under perturbation, not something with two comparable arms to run._
- **`cnn_convergence`** (absolute) — The convergence theory extends to convolutional neural networks: with sufficient over-parameterization (width polynomial in n, L, d, 1/delta), GD and SGD reach epsilon regression loss in polynomially many iterations, analogous to the fully-connected case. _Absolute convergence-rate claim tied to specific polynomial dependence on n,L,d,1/delta, not a runnable comparative experiment._
- **`resnet_convergence`** (absolute) — The convergence theory extends to residual networks: with sufficient over-parameterization (width polynomial in n, L, 1/delta), GD and SGD reach epsilon regression loss in polynomially many iterations, similar in scaling to the fully-connected case (no exponential improvement from residual connections under ReLU). _Absolute convergence-rate claim with specific polynomial iteration bound, not structured as a comparative test._
- **`classification_100_percent_accuracy`** (absolute) — For multi-label classification tasks with cross-entropy loss, over-parameterized networks trained by GD reach 100% training accuracy in a number of iterations polynomial in n, L, 1/delta. _Absolute claim about reaching exactly 100% training accuracy within a specific polynomial iteration bound, not a comparative setup._
- **`empirical_landscape_matches_theory`** (absolute) — Empirical measurements of the training landscape on CIFAR-10 with VGG19 (and other architectures/datasets in appendix) show that near the SGD trajectory, following the negative gradient direction sufficiently decreases the objective without needing second-order curvature information, consistent with the paper's theoretical almost-convexity and semi-smoothness claims even beyond the theoretically guaranteed neighborhood. _This relies on a large pretrained CNN (VGG19) on CIFAR-10 whose specific empirical landscape behavior is scale-dependent and requires substantial training beyond a CPU-only 60-minute budget, and it is an absolute/qualitative reproduction of a specific empirical figure rather than a small-scale comparative ablation._

## Limitations

Read these results narrowly.

- **A negative result here does not refute the paper.** These experiments run at a fraction of the
  original scale. An effect that disappears when the model, the data, or the training budget shrinks
  may still be real at full scale; that is precisely what reduced-scale testing cannot tell us.
- **A positive result is not a reproduction.** It says the direction of the claim survived
  shrinking, not that the paper's numbers were reproduced.
- **Seed counts are small.** With a handful of seeds per arm, statistical power is low. An
  inconclusive verdict usually means there was not enough evidence either way, not that the effect
  is absent.
- **Absolute claims are never testable this way** and are recorded without being run.
- The reduced experiments were written from the paper's description, so they may differ from the
  authors' implementation in ways that matter.
