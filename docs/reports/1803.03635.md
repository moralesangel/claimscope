## Tested claims

### winning_tickets_learn_faster_lenet

> On Lenet-300-100 for MNIST, iteratively pruned winning tickets reach early-stopping faster and with higher test accuracy than the original network, down to about 21% of weights remaining, after which further pruning slows learning.

Source: Fig. 3, Fig. 4a · Metric: early-stopping iteration and test accuracy · Expected: learning speed and accuracy increase as sparsity increases up to Pm~21%, then decrease

**Verdict: Not consistent at reduced scale**

treatment: 0.8685 (sd 0.00424, n=3); control: 0.9296 (sd 0.00642, n=3). Effect in the predicted direction: -0.06111 [95% CI -0.06759, -0.0537], Welch p=0.000377. With 3 seeds per arm this test has low power: an inconclusive result means insufficient evidence, not absence of an effect.

#### Reduction plan

_This is what was **designed**. The experiment was then written by a model from this description, so read the script in the workspace to see what actually ran -- the two can differ, for instance where the sandbox has no network and the data had to be generated instead of downloaded._

**Original setup.** On Lenet-300-100 (a 300-100 fully-connected MLP) trained on full MNIST (60k train / 10k test images), iterative magnitude pruning with weight rewinding produces "winning ticket" subnetworks. As the network is iteratively pruned down to ~21% of original weights, these winning tickets reach early-stopping (lowest validation loss) in fewer training iterations and achieve higher test accuracy than the unpruned original network trained from the same initialization; pruning below ~21% reverses this trend.

**Reduced setup.** A small fully-connected MLP with two hidden layers (sizes 50 and 25, analogous in spirit to Lenet-300-100 but scaled down for a much smaller input) trained on sklearn's 8x8 digits dataset (1797 images, 10 classes) standing in for MNIST, implemented from scratch in numpy (manual forward/backward pass, SGD, cross-entropy loss). We use only 300 training examples (out of ~1437 in an 80/20 split) to force a visible train/test gap, train for up to 200 epochs with tracking of validation loss each epoch to determine early-stopping iteration, and apply iterative magnitude pruning (unstructured, global) with weight rewinding to the original initialization, at sparsity levels {0% (baseline), 21%, 40%, 60%, 80%, 95%} of weights remaining. Two arms are compared: (A) the original unpruned network trained from a fixed initialization, and (B) iteratively pruned winning tickets rewound to that same initialization, at each sparsity level. Each arm/sparsity-level combination is run with 3 different random initializations (seeds), and for each we record the epoch of early stopping (lowest held-out validation loss, on a 20% held-out validation split of the 300 training examples used only for early-stopping detection, evaluated on a separate untouched test split of ~360 samples) and the test accuracy at that epoch.

**Why the claim should transfer.** The winning-ticket effect is claimed to arise from a structural property of pruning magnitude-based subnetworks with weight rewinding: removing near-zero-magnitude weights while keeping the surviving weights at their favorable original initialization values reduces the effective parameter count and search space the optimizer must navigate, which speeds convergence and can improve generalization up to a point, after which too few remaining weights impair the network's ability to fit the task, and both learning speed and accuracy fall off. This mechanism does not depend on MNIST's specific pixel resolution or on Lenet-300-100's exact width -- it depends only on (1) having a fully-connected network trained with SGD/backprop on a task where it can overfit given the amount of training data, and (2) applying iterative magnitude pruning with rewinding to a range of sparsities that includes both "moderate" (matching the ~21% regime) and "extreme" (over-pruned) levels. By deliberately restricting the training set to 300 of the 1797 available 8x8-digit images, we force a visible train/test accuracy gap in the unpruned baseline arm, replicating the overfitting regime the paper's MNIST setup exhibits (MNIST's 60k images are more than enough to fit Lenet-300-100's ~266k parameters, producing an analogous gap in the original paper). With that gap present, pruning has room to reduce overfitting and speed early stopping. Since the mechanism (fewer effective parameters, informative surviving weights from original init) and the regime (visible overfitting in the unpruned network) are both preserved, the qualitative direction -- speedup and accuracy gains as sparsity increases toward ~21%, decline at more extreme sparsity -- should be observable, even though the specific location of the ~21% breakpoint may itself shift with a much smaller dataset and network. If the direction does not appear at all under these conditions, that would be a meaningful negative signal about how load-bearing MNIST/Lenet-scale specifics are to the claim, rather than an artifact of failing to leave room for the effect.

**Seeds per arm:** 3 · **Estimated cost:** 50 min · **Code source:** from_scratch

**Changes from the paper**

- Dataset: sklearn 8x8 digits (1797 images) replaces MNIST (70k images) -- both are small handwritten-digit multiclass image classification tasks with a similar visual structure (digit strokes on a small grid), so the qualitative effect of sparsity on optimization dynamics and generalization should not depend on which digit dataset is used.
- Architecture: 2-layer MLP with hidden sizes 50-25 (~ (64*50+50*25+25*10) params) replaces Lenet-300-100 (300-100 hidden units for 784-dim MNIST input) -- the claim is about the general phenomenon of magnitude pruning + rewinding on a small fully-connected network, not about this specific width; keeping the same architecture family (2 hidden FC layers, same pruning procedure) preserves the mechanism while fitting an 8x8 input and CPU budget.
- Training set restricted to 300 of the available ~1437 training images -- a smaller network trained on all 1437 digits reaches near-ceiling accuracy quickly and would not show a train/test generalization gap, so the pruning-as-regularization-like effect on early stopping would have no room to appear; restricting data size recreates a regime with visible overfitting, analogous to why the original network overfits enough for pruning to matter.
- From-scratch numpy implementation of pruning and rewinding replaces the original PyTorch-based Lasso-ticket codebase -- no official repo is available and no DL framework is installed, but iterative magnitude pruning + weight rewinding to initial weights is a well-specified, framework-agnostic procedure implementable directly with numpy and does not change the underlying method.
- Sparsity levels limited to a coarse grid {0,21,40,60,80,95}% rather than the paper's dozens of iterative pruning rounds -- coarser steps still let us observe the rise-then-fall pattern in early-stopping speed/accuracy around ~21% if the same qualitative U/inverse-U-shape exists, while fitting the CPU time budget.
- Epoch budget capped at 200 with explicit early stopping via validation loss, instead of the paper's longer iteration budgets -- 200 epochs over 300 examples with a tiny MLP is enough for both convergence and overfitting to emerge on CPU within the time budget, and early-stopping detection only needs the validation loss to have turned upward, not full convergence.

**Deliberately preserved**

- The core method: unstructured global magnitude pruning done iteratively (not one-shot), with rewinding of surviving weights to their original initial values at each pruning round -- this is the exact mechanism the claim is about.
- Fully-connected feedforward MLP architecture matching the family of Lenet-300-100 (two hidden layers), rather than switching to a convolutional or other architecture.
- Comparison against the unpruned network trained from the identical initialization and same training procedure, isolating pruning+rewinding as the only difference between arms.
- Use of early-stopping iteration (lowest validation loss) and test accuracy at that iteration as the measured outcomes, matching the paper's exact metrics.
- A visible train/test generalization gap in the baseline (unpruned) arm, which is the condition under which pruning could plausibly act like an implicit regularizer/optimization aid and show the claimed speedup+accuracy gain.

### winning_ticket_beats_random_reinit_lenet

> Randomly reinitializing the weights of a winning ticket's mask (keeping the same sparse structure) causes it to learn slower and reach lower test accuracy than the original winning ticket, showing initialization is critical.

Source: Fig. 3, Fig. 4a · Metric: early-stopping iteration and test accuracy · Expected: winning_ticket > randomly_reinitialized_ticket

**Verdict: Consistent at reduced scale**

winning_ticket_original_init: 0.8619 (sd 0.0103, n=3); winning_ticket_random_reinit: 0.5279 (sd 0.0419, n=3). Effect in the predicted direction: +0.334 [95% CI +0.2888, +0.3703], Welch p=0.00349. With 3 seeds per arm this test has low power: an inconclusive result means insufficient evidence, not absence of an effect.

#### Reduction plan

_This is what was **designed**. The experiment was then written by a model from this description, so read the script in the workspace to see what actually ran -- the two can differ, for instance where the sandbox has no network and the data had to be generated instead of downloaded._

**Original setup.** Frankle & Carbin train Lenet-300-100 (a 3-layer FC network) on full MNIST (60k train images), iteratively prune to find a "winning ticket" sparse subnetwork with its original init, then compare it against the same sparse mask with weights randomly reinitialized. The winning ticket reaches equal or better test accuracy faster (lower early-stopping iteration) than the randomly reinitialized version, across multiple pruning rounds/sparsity levels.

**Reduced setup.** A hand-written numpy MLP with the same fully-connected architecture family as Lenet-300-100 but scaled down to 64-32-10 (three FC layers, ReLU), trained on sklearn's 8x8 digits dataset (1797 images total, stand-in for MNIST) using only 300 training images (to induce a visible train/test gap) and the remaining ~1497 held out for testing. Procedure: (1) train the dense network from a fixed init to convergence (~200 epochs, full-batch gradient descent, lr=0.1), (2) one-shot magnitude-prune 80% of weights globally per layer to get a sparse mask, (3) Arm A ("winning ticket"): reset the surviving sparse weights to their original initialization values and retrain from scratch with the mask fixed; (4) Arm B ("random reinit"): apply the same mask but draw fresh random initial weights (same init distribution) and retrain from scratch. Track test accuracy every 5 epochs for 200 epochs to record early-stopping epoch (epoch of best/lowest val loss or accuracy plateau) and final test accuracy. Run 3 seeds per arm, seeds controlling both the initial dense network's init and the subsequent random reinit draw (same 3 seeds reused for the initial training so masks match across arm pairs, only the reinit draw differs between arms).

**Why the claim should transfer.** The claim's mechanism -- that a specific (lucky/lottery) initialization of a sparse subnetwork trains faster and generalizes better than a fresh random initialization of the same sparse topology -- is a statement about the loss landscape induced by a given mask and a given initial point in weight space, not about network width, dataset resolution, or number of pruning rounds. As long as (a) the two arms share exactly the same sparse structure and only differ in the initial weight values, (b) the task is hard enough relative to model capacity that training dynamics and generalization actually differ across inits (ensured here by subsampling to 300 training images so the dense baseline shows a clear train/test gap, giving room for the effect to be visible in test accuracy and early-stopping epoch), and (c) both arms get identical training budget and optimizer, then any advantage of the original init should still show up qualitatively at this small scale. Since the effect in the original paper is observed to be robust across many architectures, datasets and sparsity levels (part of its claimed generality), a small FC network with 80% one-shot pruning on 8x8 digits with limited data is a reasonable regime to detect the same directional effect, even though we cannot expect identical magnitudes or exact epoch counts.

**Seeds per arm:** 3 · **Estimated cost:** 45 min · **Code source:** from_scratch

**Changes from the paper**

- Architecture width reduced from 300-100 to 64-32 hidden units -- direction of init-vs-reinit effect is a property of optimization landscape from a specific init, not of layer width, so shrinking width preserves the mechanism while cutting compute a lot.
- Dataset changed from MNIST (60k images, 28x28) to sklearn load_digits (1797 images, 8x8) explicitly as MNIST stand-in -- both are small multi-class handwritten-digit classification tasks with similar structure, so the relative comparison (same mask, different init) should behave analogously.
- Training set subsampled to 300 images (instead of using most of the 1797) -- this creates a visible train/test accuracy gap so there is genuine room for an initialization-quality effect to manifest; with too much data relative to model capacity neither arm would overfit or show a gap.
- Iterative pruning (paper's multi-round approach reaching e.g. 98%+ sparsity over many rounds) replaced by one-shot pruning to 80% sparsity -- the core claim is about init vs random-reinit at a *fixed* mask, not about the iterative procedure that finds the mask, so a single pruning step still gives a valid mask to compare both arms on.
- Full-batch gradient descent in numpy instead of SGD with momentum/Adam in the original -- optimizer choice is orthogonal to whether the original init recovers accuracy faster than a random init of the same sparse structure; full-batch GD is simplest to implement correctly from scratch and still exhibits an early-stopping curve.
- Total epochs reduced to 200 (from the paper's much longer MNIST training schedules) -- chosen so training curves visibly plateau within budget; both arms get identical epoch budget so the comparison is fair even if absolute epoch counts differ from the paper.

**Deliberately preserved**

- The core manipulation: identical sparse mask structure (same pruned architecture) compared under two initializations -- this is exactly what the claim is about.
- Fully-connected feed-forward architecture family (Lenet-style MLP), not swapped for a conv net or other method.
- Magnitude-based pruning to determine the mask, matching the paper's pruning criterion.
- Same training procedure, optimizer, learning rate, epoch budget, and dataset for both arms -- only the initialization of the sparse weights differs.
- A genuine train/test accuracy gap in the baseline (winning ticket) arm by restricting training data, so there is room for a slower/worse-performing reinit arm to be distinguishable.
- Multiple seeds (3) per arm with matched seed pairing between arms so mask identity is controlled while only init differs.

## Claims not tested

- **`winning_tickets_match_original_accuracy_smaller_size`** (absolute) — Iterative magnitude pruning finds subnetworks (winning tickets) that, when trained from their original initialization, match or exceed the test accuracy of the full network at 10-20% or less of the original parameter count. _This is an absolute headline claim about matching specific accuracy at specific sparsity levels, which cannot be validated by construction at reduced scale._
- **`iterative_pruning_beats_oneshot`** (comparative) — Iterative pruning finds smaller winning tickets that learn faster and reach higher test accuracy than one-shot pruning at comparable sparsity levels. _Both arms are simple pruning procedures on a small network/dataset, runnable within budget, and the comparative direction (iterative > one-shot) is a procedural effect expected to hold at small scale._
- **`conv_networks_winning_tickets_generalize_better`** (comparative) — For Conv-2/4/6 on CIFAR10, iteratively pruned winning tickets reach up to 3.4-3.5 percentage points higher test accuracy and up to 3.5x faster early-stopping than the original unpruned network, while training accuracy is nearly saturated for both, indicating better generalization. _Conv-2/4/6 on CIFAR10 can be shrunk (smaller subset, fewer epochs) while keeping both arms runnable on CPU, and the comparative generalization-gap direction is plausible to hold at reduced scale, though CIFAR10 CNN training is heavier than Lenet/MNIST._
- **`dropout_improves_winning_tickets_further`** (ablation) — Combining dropout with iterative pruning on Conv-2/4/6 yields additional test accuracy gains beyond what dropout or pruning alone provide, with iterative pruning adding up to 4.7 percentage points on top of dropout's initial improvement. _Both arms (dropout+pruning vs dropout alone) are reproducible on a small CNN/CIFAR10 subset within budget, and the additive-benefit direction is a plausible small-scale ablation effect, though exact percentage gains won't transfer._
- **`vgg19_winning_tickets_require_low_lr_or_warmup`** (ablation) — For VGG-19 on CIFAR10, iterative pruning fails to find winning tickets at the standard learning rate of 0.1 (performing no better than random reinitialization), but succeeds at a lower learning rate of 0.01 or with learning rate warmup at 0.1. _VGG-19 on CIFAR10 with iterative pruning across multiple LR/warmup configurations and multiple seeds is too compute-heavy for a 60-minute CPU-only budget._
- **`resnet18_winning_tickets_require_low_lr_or_warmup`** (ablation) — For Resnet-18 on CIFAR10, iterative pruning finds winning tickets at learning rate 0.01 or with learning rate warmup at higher rates, but not at the standard learning rate of 0.1 without warmup. _ResNet-18 on CIFAR10 with iterative pruning across LR schedules is too large and slow to train on CPU within the time budget._
- **`resnet18_warmup_closes_accuracy_gap`** (absolute) — Applying learning rate warmup to Resnet-18 allows winning tickets to reach the same 90.5% test accuracy as the unpruned network trained at the higher learning rate, while retaining substantial sparsity (Pm as low as 11.8%). _This is an absolute claim tied to a specific headline accuracy (90.5%) that cannot be reproduced at reduced scale, and additionally requires ResNet-18 scale training infeasible on CPU._
- **`global_pruning_finds_smaller_tickets_deep_nets`** (comparative) — For Resnet-18 and VGG-19, pruning weights globally across all convolutional layers (rather than uniformly per layer) identifies smaller winning tickets. _Both arms require training deep networks like ResNet-18/VGG-19 to convergence for iterative pruning, which exceeds the CPU-only compute budget._

Treat the results as a demonstration of the pipeline rather than as a trustworthy measurement, and re-run under Docker before relying on them.

## Limitations

Read these results narrowly.

- **A negative result here does not refute the paper.** These experiments run at a fraction of the
  original scale. An effect that disappears when the model, the data, or the training budget shrinks
  may still be real at full scale; that is precisely what reduced-scale testing cannot tell us.
- **A positive result is not a reproduction.** It says the direction of the claim survived
  shrinking, not that the paper's numbers were reproduced.
- **Seed counts are small.** With a handful of seeds per arm, statistical power is low. An
  inconclusive verdict usually means there was not enough evidence either way, not that the effect
  is absent.
- **Absolute claims are never testable this way** and are recorded without being run.
- The reduced experiments were written from the paper's description, so they may differ from the
  authors' implementation in ways that matter.
